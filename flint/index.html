Preventing Water Damage in Flint Homes

Water damage looks sudden, but the causes are usually slow: a hose that aged, a seal that dried out, a drain that clogged a little more each month. Knowing the usual suspects is most of the prevention for a Flint homeowner.

Pipes and fittings top the list. A water bill that climbs for no reason, or pressure that seems too high, can signal a concealed leak or strain on the system. Next come appliances. Run the dishwasher and the washer only while someone is in the house, and change their hoses before they show cracks. The roof and the ground outside are third. Missing shingles, cracked flashing, blocked gutters and soil that slopes toward the house all let rain find a way in.

Leaving the house empty for a while? Close the main valve before you go, or have a neighbor look in. Pontiac Restoration is glad to talk through prevention with any homeowner in Flint. If something does fail, calling quickly is what keeps the damage contained.

Restoration Challenges Flint Homeowners Face

Ask anyone on our crew what fills the schedule in Flint and you will hear the same six answers. They are all common, and with every one of them waiting adds to the repair.

Mold: Mold is usually the second chapter of a water problem that someone thought was finished. Surfaces dry with towels and a box fan, yet the pad, subfloor and inside of the wall hold moisture. Growth can begin within 24 to 48 hours, then spread quietly until you smell it or see spotting.

Hoses: Every sink, toilet and washer is fed by a flexible connector that holds full house pressure around the clock. A burst connector keeps spraying until somebody closes a valve, and that may take a long while in an empty house. The low-cost defense is to swap out aging hoses and learn where the main shutoff is.

Pumps: Sump pumps, where a home has one, tend to fail at the worst moment after years of running without attention. What we usually see is a jammed float, a motor that has worn out or a breaker that tripped. The result is several inches of water over the lowest floor, soaking stored boxes, flooring and the bottom of the walls. Testing the pump now and then, and adding a battery backup, lowers the odds considerably.

Toilets: An overflowing toilet is the most common bathroom emergency we handle in Flint. It matters where the water came from, because tank or supply water is clean and used bowl water is not. In both cases it seeps beneath cabinets and flooring edges, and a moisture meter shows how far it went.

Cooling: Your air conditioner drains condensation through a line that most owners never see. If the line plugs or the pan rusts through, water drips into the ceiling or the floor around the unit. Because it happens slowly, the damage in many Flint homes is well along before a stain appears. Having the line flushed each year, before the cooling season, prevents most of these.

Flooding: A long, heavy rain can overwhelm street drains and send water across the yard and into the house. Garages, walkout basements and ground-floor rooms at the low side of the lot flood first. We pump it out, discard materials that cannot be sanitized and keep equipment running until moisture readings are back to normal.
